Mechanical Design & Powertrain

Dual Ratio Passive-Shifting Transmission

A mechanical powertrain project focused on designing and fabricating a two-speed transmission for a bicycle wheel capable of shifting gear ratios automatically without electronic actuators or complex microcontroller supervision. The system leverages one-way roller clutches to shift between a 9:1 high-torque ratio and a 4:1 high-speed ratio simply by reversing the direction of the input motor. This was developed as part of the Transmission Design Competition for ME13 (Design and Fabrication) at Caltech, and our team of 6 ended up winning the competition, setting a new record.

Mechanical Principles & Architecture

Standard multi-speed transmissions rely on solenoid actuators or active clutches to switch gears. This design eliminates active shifting components by integrating one-way roller clutches into the parallel gear trains.

When the input motor spins in the forward direction, the high-torque (9:1) roller clutch engages the torque shaft, driving the output while the high-speed gear train freewheels. To shift, a DPDT switch reverses the motor polarity. As the motor spins in reverse, the high-torque clutch disengages and the high-speed (5.04:1) roller clutch engages. This allows for instantaneous, mechanical shifting by simply toggling the motor direction.

Design & Analysis Highlights

  • Gear-Belt Solver Optimization: Driving parallel gear and timing belt stages from the same fixed shafts requires exact center-to-center distances to prevent binding or slack. A custom solver was developed to evaluate all possible gear and pulley tooth combinations, identifying a configuration that utilized a 41T GT3 3mm belt with a center distance of 42.127mm, resulting in a negligible 0.0116mm of belt slack without requiring a tensioner.
  • Keyless Press-Fit Assembly: To simplify fabrication and avoid the significant machining required for keyed shafts, the design uses interference press-fits and set screws. Lame equations were utilized to calculate the necessary interference, proving the press-fits could survive tangential and radial loads with a safety factor of 13, and friction torque with a safety factor of 2.
  • Material Selection and MOI Reduction: Reducing the moment of inertia was a primary design goal to minimize power losses. The gear train was designed using full Acetal rather than brass. To ensure structural integrity under the high-speed tangential loading, the width of the 25-tooth acetal gear was doubled, yielding a safety factor of 2.02 against shear failure.
  • FEA Structural Validation: Finite element stress analysis was conducted on the high-torque output shaft under combined loading. The maximum von Mises stress reached 13.7 MPa, well within the material limits and notably lower than the stress concentrations typically observed in keyed shaft equivalents.

Background

The development of the Dual-Ratio Transmission centered around a co-designed mechanism that utilized one-way roller clutches to shift gear ratios by reversing the motor direction. The drivetrain design was validated before manufacturing using MATLAB, hand calculations, and ANSYS Mechanical FEA to optimize gear ratios, refine shift timing, and determine shaft geometry (which accounted for frictional losses and shaft deformation under load). Following the design and validation phase, we moved into production, involving engineering drawing creation, component machining, and complete assembly of the drivetrain.

During prototyping, testing was required to evaluate the shifting mechanism. I managed the electromechanical integration for these initial tests by wiring a Double-Pole Double-Throw (DPDT) switch for polarity reversal, connecting the motor directly to the switch's two middle pins. This allowed us to trigger the motor reversal required for shifting. However, physical testing revealed slip between the aluminum and acetal components. To resolve this, we added aluminum plates and hubs to the acetal gears, enabling a metal-on-metal press fit onto the roller clutches, eliminating the slipping issue.

Just before the competition, we determined that top-end performance needed enhancement, prompting a change in the high-speed gear ratio from 5.04:1 to 4:1. Since the transmission utilized 3D-printed pulleys, we could switch pulley sizes quickly to accommodate this ratio change without manufacturing delays. Ultimately, this drivetrain design improved the vehicle's overall acceleration by 18%, allowing the team to win 1st place and set a competition record.
